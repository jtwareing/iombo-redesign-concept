About Us

Scotland’s longest running bird observatory

The Observatory was founded by a group of young Scottish ornithologists and has continued to depend on the enthusiasm of amateurs who come to the island, usually for a week at a time, to maintain observations.

Who we are

The Isle of May hosts Scotland’s oldest bird observatory, founded in 1934. The observatory is administered by a charitable trust, The Isle of May Bird Observatory and Field Station Trust, a Scottish Charitable Incorporated Organisation (Scottish Charity Number SC001783), and manned by visiting volunteer observers between March and November.

The Observatory is run by a management committee on a voluntary basis.

The island, sitting 5 miles off the Fife coast in eastern Scotland, is a National Nature Reserve, owned and managed by NatureScot (opens in a new tab), and is internationally important for its populations of breeding seabirds, particularly Puffins Fratercula arctica.

Our mission

The Isle of May Bird Observatory facilitates the monitoring and study of bird migration and other aspects of ornithology and natural history, by anyone with an interest in the natural world. In conjunction with partners, it contributes to bird conservation and science through its activities, it provides opportunities for ornithological training and education for all, in safe and welcoming facilities, at events and through the media. It enables enjoyment of, and participation in, the study of the natural world.

Since 1934

Scotland’s first observatory

The chatty log begins on 28th September 1934 with the arrival of a party of 4: W B Alexander, R M Lockley, HFD Elder and EV Watson, great names of 20th Century ornithology. Among early activity they started building a Heligoland trap.

The chatty logs, held in the observatory, provide an informal account of the observatory’s years of operation, and the occasional extract appears on the Chatty Blog.

Facilities

The Low Light

The accommodation, for up to 6 people, is housed within the Low Light, a former navigational lighthouse. A major redevelopment took place in 2014 and work continues to maintain and upgrade the facilities every year.

The observatory, a listed building, sleeps six in three bedrooms. There is a ‘boot room’, a comfortable lounge with woodburning stove and well stocked ornithological library, a small ‘office’ space, and a dining kitchen. Electric lighting comes from solar panels. And the outside space includes the best views for many miles.
